# Debrief: read after you open the envelope

## 1. Your score

**Weighted service-hours lost** is your headline score:

> for every end-use facility, hours out × its weight, all added up.

**Unweighted hours out** sits beside it: the same hours with every facility counted the same.

Lower is better on both. The two can rank outcomes differently. Ten hours without a cell mast (weight 1) and ten hours without the main hospital (weight 10) count the same in unweighted hours. On the weighted score the hospital costs ten times as much. **The weights decide what counts as the worst outcome**, and choosing them is a judgment about what a community needs most. The game uses the Lifeline weights, which put life-safety services first.

## 2. Compare, line by line

Lay your sheets beside the envelope's:
1. **Crew sheet against the engine's timeline.** Same jobs, same order, same times?
2. **Event log.** Find the first event where you and the engine differ. Everything after it may follow from that one difference, so fix the first and re-check the rest.
3. **Outage sheet.** Facility by facility: same facilities out, same hours?
4. **Every difference gets a cause,** written on the feedback sheet:
   - **a slip:** an arithmetic or reading error; the pack was clear;
   - **an unclear pack:** a rule card or node card could fairly be read your way. Your decision log should show it;
   - **the model:** you followed the cards exactly and the engine did something else. This is the most valuable finding of all, because it means the model needs a second look.

## 3. Why this matters

You just did what a planner must do after a storm: follow damage through dependencies to see who loses service, for how long, and what the crews can do about it. You did it knowing everything: every dependency, every back-up, every rule.

A real planner doesn't know everything. Public sources show where the facilities are, but rarely who depends on whom. So a planner often works from a **flattened** picture: the facilities, with the connections between networks guessed or left out. The research behind this game asks how much that costs: how much worse are the decisions made from a flattened picture than from one that **carries** the dependencies? It does so on worlds like this island, where the true answer is known.

Your trace, compared with the engine's, is the first check of that machinery: a person, working from the cards alone, should reach the same answer the computer does. Where you don't, you've found something we need to know.
